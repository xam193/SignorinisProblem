\section{Introduction}\label{sec:Introduction}


We are given a body $\Omega\subset\R^d$. While all of the below may be formulated for arbitrary $d\ge 1$, it is of course reasonable to restrict our selfs to the real world application $d\in\{2,3\}$. Further necessary topological assumptions on $\Omega$ are introduced in the next subsection. This body may be sitting on a {\em rigid foundation}. See Figure \ref{fig:setup_plain} (left). Furthermore, let our body be made of an {\em elastic material}, allowing $\Omega$ to deform due to {\em internal} and external {\em forces}. If these forces are large enough, $\Omega$ in its initial state as in Figure \ref{fig:setup_plain} (left) is not in a {\em balanced} configuration. The internal and external forces are not in equilibrium. If we now allow all forces to act until the body is in an equilibrium configuration, this could look like Figure \ref{fig:setup_plain} (right). The body Omega in this state is designated $\varphi(\Omega)$.

\begin{figure}[!h]
	\centering
	\input{Figures/setup_plain.tex}
	\caption{Body $\Omega$ on a rigid foundation in initial (left) and equilibrium (right) configuration.}
	\label{fig:setup_plain}
\end{figure}

The transition of $\Omega$ in its initial configuration into the $\Omega$ in its equilibrium configuration under prescribed internal and external forces defines the map
\begin{equation*}
	\varphi:\Omega\to \R^d,\qquad \varphi(x) = x+ u(x),
\end{equation*}
where $u: \Omega\to \R^d$ is called the {\em displacement} function. The Signorini Problem seeks precisely this function under given elasticity properties, forces, boundary conditions and other rules.


\section{Geometrical and Physical Setup}

Naturally, all of the above must be mathematically modeled in order to apply suitable theories and methods, e.g.\ from functional analysis. This will be covered in the following sections. Throughout, $\Omega\subset \R^d,\, d\in\{2,3\}$ denotes an open, bounded and connected domain with boundary $\Gamma:=\partial\Omega$, that is at least Lipschitz, i.e.\ $\Gamma\in \cC^{m,1},\, m\ge 0$. Among other things, this provides us with an {\em outward normal} $\mathrm{n}\in\cC^{m-1,1}(\Gamma;\R^d)$ if $m = 1$. For now, $m = 0$ is sufficient Then, $\mathrm{n}\in L^\infty(\Omega;\R^d)$. Later on, higher regularity on $\Gamma$ is needed. We begin by dividing the boundary into different sub-boundaries, which will later each be subject to different boundary conditions. Then the elasticity properties of the body are appropriately modeled and we discuss how the acting forces are understood. Finally, it is defined which shift functions are admissible at all.

\subsection{Boundary Decomposition}

The boundary is decomposed into three pairwise disjoint parts
\begin{equation*}
	\Gamma = \overline{\Gamma_D}\,\mathbin{\dot\cup}\,\overline{\Gamma_N}\,\mathbin{\dot\cup}\,\overline{\Gamma_C},\qquad |\Gamma_C|,|\Gamma_D|>0,\;|\Gamma_N|\ge 0.
\end{equation*}
which are assumed to be open in $\Gamma$ and where each part is subject to different boundary conditions to model different physical interactions (See Figure \ref{fig:setup}).

\begin{figure}[!h]
	\centering
	\input{Figures/setup.tex}
	\caption{Decomposition of $\Gamma$ in Dirichlet, Neumann and Contact parts before (left) and after displacement (right).}
	\label{fig:setup}
\end{figure}

While $\Gamma_C$ and $\Gamma_D$ are assumed to be of positive $(d-1)$-Lebesgue measure, $\Gamma_N$ may in some instances be a null set, or more roughly speaking, nonexistent. On $\Gamma_D$, we apply {\em homogeneous Dirichlet} boundary conditions. This forces the body to be {\em clamped} and this part, i.e.\ the displacement should not act on $\Gamma_D$. The part $\Gamma_N$ may be subject to a {\em Neumann} boundary condition, which means we impose a known {\em surface force density}. $\Gamma_C$ denotes the {\em potential contact} boundary.  The rigid foundation is  positioned so that any actual contact, both initially and in the deformed equilibrium configuration, may only occur on $\Gamma_C$. While $\Gamma_C$ is prescribed a priori, the {\em active contact zone} $\Gamma_{\mathrm{act}}(u)\subset\Gamma_C$ is unknown and depends on the solution $u$. Later on, the boundary conditions imposed on $\Gamma_C$ enforce {\em unilateral contact} through {\em nonpenetration} and {\em complementarity}. The rigid foundation can be modeled through a {\em gap-function} $g:\Gamma_C\to\R$, describing the distance between $\Gamma_C$ and the foundation. While the foundation in Figure \ref{fig:setup} suggests a piece-wise constant gap-function $g$, we will throughout the whole report assume $g = 0$.

\subsection{Basic Notations on Sobolev Spaces}

This subsection is intended to fix some of the notations used in this report. Since Signorini's problem is formulated as a search for the displacement function as a partial differential equation, Sobolev spaces are of particular importance. The Sobolev space $W^{k,p}(\Omega;\R^d)$ contains every $L^p(\Omega;\R^d)$-function, such that weak derivatives up to order $k\ge 0$ are again in $L^p(\Omega;\R^d)$. Here, we are only interested in the Hilbert space case $p= 2$ and we write $H^k(\Omega;\R^d):= W^{k,2}(\Omega;\R^d)$. The $L^2$-inner product and norm is denoted by $(\cdot,\,\cdot)_{0,\Omega}$ and $\|\cdot\|_{0,\Omega}$. We record this introduction in the following definition and simultaneously establish a notation for the associated norm.

\begin{definition}[Sobolev Spaces of Integer Order]
	Let $k\in\N_0$. The {\tt Sobolev space} $H^k(\Omega;\R^d)$ is defined by
	\begin{equation*}
		H^k(\Omega;\R^d)
		:=
		\left\{u\in L^2(\Omega;\R^d)\;\middle|\; \Diff^\alpha u\in L^2(\Omega;\R^d)\ \forall\,\alpha\in\N_0^d,\ |\alpha|\le k\right\},
	\end{equation*}
	where $\Diff^\alpha u$ is understood in the weak sense. Moreover, we equip $H^k(\Omega;\R^d)$ with the norm
	\begin{equation*}
		\|u\|_{k,\Omega}
		:=
		\left(
		\sum_{|\alpha|\le k}\|\Diff^\alpha u\|_{0,\Omega}^2
		\right)^{1/2},
		\qquad u\in H^k(\Omega;\R^d).
	\end{equation*}
\end{definition}
We adopt the usual convention $H^0(\Omega;\R^d) = L^2(\Omega;\R^d)$ explaining the notion of the $L^2$-norm. Furthermore, we state that $H^k(\Omega;\R^d)$ is a Hilbert space.

\begin{proposition}[$H^k(\Omega;\R^d)$ is a Hilbert Space]
	The Sobolev space $H^k(\Omega;\R^d)$ together with the inner product
	\begin{equation*}
		(u,\, v)_{k,\Omega}:= \sum_{|\alpha|\le k}(\Diff^\alpha u,\, \Diff^\alpha v)_{0,\Omega},\qquad u,v\in H^k(\Omega;\R^d)
	\end{equation*}
	is a Hilbert space and the induced the norm $\|\cdot\|_{k,\Omega}$.
	\eop
\end{proposition}

After the integer-order spaces $H^k(\Omega;\R^d)$, we also need Sobolev spaces of non-integer order. The main reason is that trace operators naturally lead to boundary spaces of fractional order. In particular, traces of $H^1(\Omega;\R^d)$-functions are, in general, not measured in another integer-order Sobolev space, but in a space of order $1/2$ on the boundary and similarly on boundary parts $\Gamma_0\subset\Gamma$. For a non-integer order $s=k+\theta$ with $\theta\in(0,1)$, the remaining fractional part $\theta$ is measured by difference quotients. More precisely, one measures how strongly a function oscillates between pairs of points $x,y\in\Omega$, with a weight that becomes singular as $|x-y|\to 0$. This leads to the following quantity.

\begin{definition}[Slobodeckij Seminorm]
	Let $m\ge 1$ and $\theta\in(0,1)$. For a measurable function $u:\Omega\to \R^d$, we define the {\tt Slobodeckij semi-norm}
	\begin{equation*}
		|u|_{\theta,\Omega}
		:=
		\left(\int_\Omega\int_\Omega \frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^2}{|x-y|^{d+2\theta}}\;\dx\;\dy\right)^{1/2},
	\end{equation*}
	whenever the right-hand side is finite.
\end{definition}

The quantity $|u|_{\theta,\Omega}$ is only a semi-norm, since it vanishes on constant functions. Combined with an $L^2$-term, however, it yields a genuine norm. With this, we can define Sobolev spaces of fractional order and just as above, state, that this provides us with a Hilbert space together with suitable inner product.

\begin{definition}[Sobolev Spaces of Fractional Order]\label{def:fractionalsobolev}
	Let $s=k+\theta$ with $k\in\N_0$ and $\theta\in(0,1)$. The {\tt Sobolev space} of fractional order $s$ is defined by
	\begin{equation*}
		H^s(\Omega;\R^d)
		:=
		\left\{
		u\in H^k(\Omega;\R^d)\;\middle|\; |\Diff^\alpha u|_{\theta,\Omega}<\infty \ \forall\,\alpha\in\N_0^d,\ |\alpha|=k
		\right\},
	\end{equation*}
	where $\Diff^\alpha u$ is understood in the weak sense. Moreover, we equip $H^s(\Omega;\R^d)$ with the norm
	\begin{equation*}
		\|u\|_{s,\Omega}
		:=
		\left(
		\|u\|_{k,\Omega}^2 + |u|_{s,\Omega}^2
		\right)^{1/2},
		\qquad u\in H^s(\Omega;\R^d).
	\end{equation*}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[$H^s(\Omega;\R^d)$ is a Hilbert Space]
	Let $s=k+\theta$ with $k\in\N_0$ and $\theta\in(0,1)$. Then $H^s(\Omega;\R^d)$ is a Hilbert space with inner product
	\begin{equation*}
		(u,\,v)_{s,\Omega}
		:=
		(u,\,v)_{k,\Omega}
		+
		\sum_{|\alpha|=k}
		\int_\Omega\int_\Omega
		\frac{\big(\Diff^\alpha u(x)-\Diff^\alpha u(y)\big)\cdot\big(\Diff^\alpha v(x)-\Diff^\alpha v(y)\big)}
		{|x-y|^{d+2\theta}}\;\dx\;\dy
	\end{equation*}
	for $u,v\in H^s(\Omega;\R^d)$. The induced norm is $\|\cdot\|_{s,\Omega}$.
	\eop
\end{proposition}

For $u\in H^s(\Omega;\R^d)$, pointwise boundary values are in general not defined. The trace theorem provides a canonical replacement.

\begin{definition}[Classical Trace on Smooth Functions]
	We define the {\tt classical trace operator}
	\begin{equation*}
		\gamma_0:\cC^\infty(\overline{\Omega};\R^d)\to L^2(\Gamma;\R^d),
		\qquad
		\gamma_0 u:=u|_\Gamma
	\end{equation*}
	on the space
	\begin{equation*}
		\cC^\infty(\overline{\Omega};\R^d)
		:=
		\left\{u|_{\overline{\Omega}}\;\middle|\;u\in \cC^\infty(U;\R^d)\text{\em\enspace for some open neighborhood }U\supset\overline{\Omega}\right\}.
	\end{equation*}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Boundary Sobolev Spaces]\label{def:boundary_sobolev}
	Let $s>1/2$. We define
	\begin{equation*}
		H^{s-1/2}(\Gamma;\R^d):=\gamma_0\big(\cC^\infty(\overline{\Omega};\R^d)\big)
	\end{equation*}
	equipped with the norm
	\begin{equation*}
		\|g\|_{s-\tfrac12,\Gamma}
		:=
		\inf\left\{
		\|u\|_{s,\Omega}
		\;\middle|\;
		u\in \cC^\infty(\overline{\Omega};\R^d),\ \gamma_0 u=g
		\right\}.
	\end{equation*}
\end{definition}

Notice how the underlying set is the same for different values of $s>1/2$, but it is equipped with different norms.

\begin{theorem}[Trace Theorem]\label{thm:trace}
	Let $\Gamma\in \cC^{m,1},\, m\ge 0$ and $1/2< s\le m+1$. Then the classical trace operator $\gamma_0$ extends uniquely to a continuous surjective linear operator
	\begin{equation*}
		\gamma:H^s(\Omega;\R^d)\to H^{s-1/2}(\Gamma;\R^d).
	\end{equation*}
	In particular, there exists a constant $C_{\tr}>0$ such that
	\begin{equation*}
		\|\gamma u\|_{s-\tfrac12,\Gamma}\le C_{\tr}\|u\|_{s,\Omega}
		\qquad\forall\,u\in H^s(\Omega;\R^d).
	\end{equation*}\eop
\end{theorem}
In the special case $s=1$, we further have
\begin{equation*}
	\ker(\gamma)=\overline{\cD(\Omega;\R^d)}^{\|\cdot\|_{1,\Omega}}=:H_0^1(\Omega;\R^d),
\end{equation*}
where $\cD(\Omega;\R^d)$ denotes the space of $\R^d$-valued test functions on $\Omega$.

\subsection{Elasticity and Forces}\label{sec:elastforce}

In Elasticity, stresses are described by {\em symmetric second order tensors},
\begin{equation*}
	\S^d:= \left\{s\in\R^d\otimes\R^d\;\middle|\; s^\top = s\right\}.
\end{equation*}
We identify the tensor space by symmetric $d\times d$-matrices in the canonical basis. Indeed,
\begin{equation*}
	\S^d\cong \left\{ S\in \R^{d\times d}\;\middle|\; S^\top = S\right\}.
\end{equation*}
As an inner product on $\R^{d\times d}$, we use the standard Frobenius product
\begin{equation*}
	A:B:= \sum_{j = 1}^d\sum_{k = 1}^d A_{jk}\, B_{jk},\qquad |A|:= \sqrt{A:A}
\end{equation*}
for real $d\times d$-matrices $A$ and $B$, while for $x,y\in \R^d$ we denote by
\begin{equation*}
	x\cdot y:= \sum_{j = 1}^d x_j\, y_j,\qquad |x|:= \sqrt{x\cdot y},
\end{equation*}
the Euclidean inner product and norm. As $\S^d$ may be identified with a subspace of $\R^{d^2}$, the above definitions of Sobolev spaces may be naturally extended to $\S^d$-valued functions on $\Omega$. We continue by defining the {\em small strain tensor} for a displacement function $u\in H^1(\Omega;\R^d)$, which is similar to the symmetric part of square matrices but for the gradient $\nabla u$ of $u$ and through which the deformation of the body $\overline{\Omega}$ by $u$ is quantified.

\begin{definition}[Small Strain Tensor]
	The {\tt (small) strain tensor} $\varepsilon$ is defined by
	\begin{equation*}
		\varepsilon:H^s(\Omega;\R^d)\to H^{s-1}(\Omega;\S^d),\qquad u\mapsto \varepsilon(u):= \frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla u + \nabla u^\top\right).
	\end{equation*}
\end{definition}

In linear elasticity, stresses are assumed to depend linearly on strains. The material response is encoded in the fourth order {\em elasticity tensor} $\cA$, which maps a symmetric strain tensor to the corresponding {\em Cauchy stress tensor}. The tensor field $\cA$ is prescribed by the material and may vary in space, describing a {\em heterogeneous} body. For a fourth oder tensor $\cA:\Omega\to\cL(\S^d;\S^d)$ and $s\in \S^d$, we use the {\em double contraction}
\begin{equation*}
	(\cA(x):s)_{ij}:=\sum_{k = 1}^d \sum_{l = 1}^d \cA_{ijkl}(x)s_{kl},
	\qquad
	s:\cA(x):s:=\sum_{i = 1}^d \sum_{j = 1}^d \sum_{k = 1}^d \sum_{l = 1}^d\cA_{ijkl}(x)s_{ij}s_{kl}.
\end{equation*}

\begin{definition}[Elasticity Tensor Field]\label{def:elastfield}
	An {\tt elasticity tensor field} is a fourth order tensor
	\begin{equation*}
		\cA:\Omega\to \cL(\S^d;\S^d)
	\end{equation*}
	such that the coefficients satisfy the symmetries
	\begin{equation*}
		\cA_{ijkl}(x)=\cA_{jikl}(x)=\cA_{ijlk}(x)=\cA_{klij}(x),
		\qquad \forall\,1\le i,j,k,l\le d,
	\end{equation*}
	and the uniform ellipticity condition, i.e.\ there exists $\alpha_\cA>0$ such that for a.e.\ $x\in\Omega$
	\begin{equation*}
		s:\cA(x):s \ge \alpha_\cA\, (s:s)\qquad \forall\,s\in \S^d.
	\end{equation*}
	Moreover, there exists $C_\cA>0$ such that for a.e.\ $x\in\Omega$
	\begin{equation*}
		|\cA_{ijkl}(x)|\le C_\cA \qquad \forall\,1\le i,j,k,l\le d.
	\end{equation*}
\end{definition}

The {\em internal} and {\em surface forces} in the body $\overline{\Omega}$ are described by the {\em Cauchy stress tensor}.

\begin{definition}[Cauchy Stress Tensor]
	We define the {\tt (linear) Cauchy stress tensor} by
	\begin{equation*}
		\sigma: H^{s}(\Omega;\R^d)\to H^{s-1}(\Omega;\S^d),\qquad u\mapsto \cA : \varepsilon(u),
	\end{equation*}
	where $\cA$ is a sufficiently smooth elasticity tensor field, such that $\sigma$ is well-defined.
\end{definition}

In the case $s = 1$, it is sufficient to require $\cA_{ijkl}\in L^\infty(\Omega)$ for every $1\le i,j,k,l\le d$ for $\sigma(u) = \cA:\varepsilon(u)$ to be part of $L^2(\Omega;\S^d)$ for every $u\in H^1(\Omega;\R^d)$. Later on, higher order Sobolev spaces are asked for, which requires higher regularity of $\cA$. For example, for $3/2<s\le 2$ it is sufficient if $\cA_{ijkl}\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)$ for every $1\le i,j,k,l\le d$, while constant $\cA$ always lead to well-defined stress tensors $\sigma$.

The function $f\in L^2(\Omega;\R^d)$ may describe a {\em volume force density} and $F\in L^2(\Gamma_N;\R^d)$ may represent {\em surface loads} on the Neumann boundary $\Gamma_N$. With the basic principle that work is force times displacement, it is reasonable to define the linear functional
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loadfunc}
	\ell(v):= \int_\Omega f\cdot v\;\dx +\int_{\Gamma_N} F\cdot(\gamma v)\;\ds ,\qquad v\in H^1(\Omega;\R^d),
\end{equation}
describing the {\em virtual work of external forces}. As he internal forces are described by the stress $\sigma(u)= \cA:\varepsilon(u)$, the same principle suggests the symmetric bilinear form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:symbil}
	a(u,v):= \int_\Omega \sigma(u):\varepsilon(u)\;\dx,\,\qquad u,v\in H^1(\Omega;\R^d).
\end{equation}
as a representative for the {\em virtual work of internal forces}. Equilibrium between both reads $a(u,v) = \ell(v)$ for all $v\in H^1(\Omega;\R^d)$, which already looks like a weak formulation but at this point, only the Neumann boundary conditions on $\Gamma_N$ are imposed. In the following, we want to prove standard properties of $a$ and $\ell$ that are needed later on. For this, we will need the following inequalities. The first inequality is a result of {\em Korn's second inequality} (\cite[Theorem 3.3]{book} and \cite[Section 3.5.2]{book}) and the {\em Peetre--Tartar} Lemma~(\cite[Lemma~3.5]{book}). Therefore only the proof of the second one is given.
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:korn}
	There exists a constant $C_K>0$ such that
	\begin{equation*}
		C_K^{-1}\,\|v\|_{1,\Omega} \le \|\varepsilon(v)\|_{0,\Omega}\le \|v\|_{1,\Omega} \qquad \forall\, v\in H^1(\Omega;\R^d).
	\end{equation*}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	We prove the second inequality. For all $v\in H^1(\Omega;\R^d)$ we have
	\begin{equation*}
		|\varepsilon(v)(x)| = \left|\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla v(x) +\nabla v^\top\right)\right|\le \frac{1}{2}\left(|\nabla v(x)|+|\nabla v(x)^\top|\right) = |\nabla v(x)|
	\end{equation*}
	and thus
	\begin{equation*}
		\|\varepsilon(v)\|_{0,\Omega}^2 =\int_\Omega |\varepsilon(v)(x)|^2\;\dx \le\int_\Omega |\nabla v(x)|^2\;\dx = \|\nabla v\|_{0,\Omega}^2\le \|v\|_{1,\Omega}^2,
	\end{equation*}
	where the last inequality holds due to
	\begin{equation*}
		\|v\|_{1,\Omega}^2 = \|v\|_{0,\Omega}^2 + \|\nabla v\|_{0,\Omega}^2.
	\end{equation*}
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{pr:aproperties}
	The symmetric bilinear form $a:H^1(\Omega;\R^d)\times H^1(\Omega;\R^d)\to \R$ defined in \eqref{eq:symbil} is
	\begin{itemize}
		\item[i)] {\em bounded}, i.e.\ for all $v,w\in H^1(\Omega;\R^d)$ there exists $C_1<\infty$ such that
		      \begin{equation*}
			      |a(u,v)|\le C_1\,\|u\|_{1,\Omega}\,\|v\|_{1,\Omega}\qquad \text{with}\qquad C_1 = C_\cA,
		      \end{equation*}
		\item[ii)] $V${\em -elliptic}, i.e.\ for all $v\in H^1(\Omega;\R^d)\setminus\{0\}$ there exist $C_2>0$ such that
		      \begin{equation*}
			      a(v,v)\ge C_2\,\|v\|_{1,\Omega}^2\qquad\text{with}\qquad C_2 = \frac{\alpha_\cA}{C_K^2}.
		      \end{equation*}
	\end{itemize}
	Moreover, the linear functional $\ell$ defined in \eqref{eq:loadfunc} is {\em bounded}, i.e.\ for all $v\in H^1(\Omega;\R^d)$ there exists $C_3>0$ such that
	\begin{equation}\label{eq:ellbound}
		|\ell (v)|\le C_3\,\|v\|_{1,\Omega}\qquad\text{with}\qquad C_3 = \max\{1,C_\mathrm{tr}\}\left(\|f\|_{0,\Omega}+\|F\|_{0,\Gamma_N}\right)
	\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
	Let $v,w\in H^1(\Omega;\R^d)$ be arbitrary. Then
	\begin{equation*}
		|a(v,w)|\le \|\cA:\varepsilon(v)\|_{0,\Omega}\,\|\varepsilon(w)\|_{0,\Omega}
	\end{equation*}
	by the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality. With Definition \ref{def:elastfield} there exists $C_\cA>0$ such that
	\begin{equation*}
		\|\cA:\varepsilon(v)\|_{0,\Omega}\le C_\cA\,\|\varepsilon(v)\|_{0,\Omega}.
	\end{equation*}
	Combining this with the second inequality form Lemma \ref{lem:korn} we have
	\begin{equation*}
		|a(v,w)|\le C_\cA\,\|\varepsilon(v)\|_{0,\Omega}\,\|\varepsilon(w)\|_{0,\Omega}\le C_\cA\,\|v\|_{1,\Omega}\,\|w\|_{1,\Omega}.
	\end{equation*}
	The ellipticity of $a$ follows by
	\begin{equation*}
		a(v,v) = \int_\Omega \varepsilon(v):\cA:\varepsilon(v)\;\dx \ge \alpha_\cA\int_\Omega\varepsilon(v):\varepsilon(v) = \alpha_\cA\,\|\varepsilon(v)\|^2_{0,\Omega},
	\end{equation*}
	where $\alpha_\cA>0$ exists due to Definition \ref{def:elastfield}. The first inequality of Lemma \ref{lem:korn} finally yields
	\begin{equation*}
		a(v,v)\ge \alpha_\cA\,\|\varepsilon(v)\|_{0,\Omega}^2\ge \alpha_\cA\,\left(C_K^{-1}\,\|v\|_{1,\Omega}\right)^2 = \frac{\alpha_\cA}{C_K^2}\,\|v\|_{1,\Omega}^2.
	\end{equation*}
	Again by Cauchy--Schwarz inequality,
	\begin{equation*}
		|\ell(v)|\le \|f\|_{0,\Omega}\,\|v\|_{0,\Omega}+\|F\|_{0,\Gamma_N}\,\|\gamma v\|_{0,\Gamma_N}\qquad \forall\, v\in H^1(\Omega;\R^d).
	\end{equation*}
	By the Trace Theorem \ref{thm:trace} there exists $C_\mathrm{tr}>0$ such that
	\begin{equation*}
		\|\gamma v\|_{0,\Gamma_N}\le \|\gamma v\|_{0,\Gamma}\le \|\gamma v\|_{\tfrac12,\Gamma}\le C_\mathrm{tr}\,\|v\|_{1,\Omega} \qquad \forall\, v\in H^1(\Omega;\R^d).
	\end{equation*}
	Finally,
	\begin{equation*}
		|\ell(v)|\le \left(\|f\|_{0,\Omega} + C_{\mathrm{tr}}\,\|F\|_{0,\Gamma_N}\right)\|v\|_{1,\Omega}\le \max\{1,C_\mathrm{tr}\}\,\left(\|f\|_{0,\Omega}+\|F\|_{0,\Gamma_N}\right)\|v\|_{1,\Omega}
	\end{equation*}
	for every $v\in H^1(\Omega;\R^d)$, which proves \eqref{eq:ellbound}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Admissible Displacements}

We start by introducing the underlying Hilbert space $V$ in which homogeneous dirichlet boundary conditions on $\Gamma_D$ are already incorporated by
\begin{equation*}
	V:= \left\{v\in H^1(\Omega;\R^d)\;\middle|\; (\gamma v)|_{\Gamma_D} = 0\right\}\subset H^1(\Omega;\R^d).
\end{equation*}
For $v\in V$ we define the {\em normal component} of the boundary displacement on $\Gamma$ by $v_\mathrm{n}:= (\gamma v)\cdot\mathrm{n}$. Here, since $\Gamma$ is Lipschitz, $\Gamma\in \cC^{0,1}$, the outward normal $\mathrm{n}$ belongs to $L^\infty(\Omega;\R^d)$ which yields $v_\mathrm{n}\in H^{1/2}(\Gamma)$. The rigid foundation is located on the exterior side of $\Gamma_C$ in direction of $\mathrm n$. Hence the body is not allowed to move across $\Gamma_C$ into the obstacle. This yields that the solution must satisfy the {\em nonpenetration} condition
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nonpenetration}
	u_{\mathrm n}\le 0 \qquad \text{a.e.\ on }\Gamma_C.
\end{equation}
This motivates the nonempty, closed and convex cone of {\em admissible displacements}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:admissible}
	K := \left\{ v\in V\;\middle|\; v_{\mathrm n}\le 0 \text{ a.e.\ on }\Gamma_C\right\}\subset V.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[$K$ is a closed convex cone]\label{pr:K}
	The set $K$ defined in \ref{eq:admissible} is a nonempty, closed and convex cone in $V$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
	$K$ is nonempty, since $0\in K$. Let $\lambda\in[0,\infty)$ and $v\in K\subset V$. Since $V$ is a vector space, we have $\lambda v\in V$. Furthermore,
	\begin{equation*}
		(\lambda v)_\mathrm{n} = \gamma(\lambda v)\cdot \mathrm{n} = (\lambda\gamma v)\cdot \mathrm{n} = \lambda(\gamma v\cdot n) = \lambda v_\mathrm{n}.
	\end{equation*}
	Since $v_\mathrm{n}\le 0$ a.e.\ on $\Gamma_C$ and $\lambda\ge 0$, we also have $\lambda v_\mathrm{n}\le 0$ a.e.\ on $\Gamma_C$, i.e.\ $\lambda v\in K$. Thus $K$ is a cone in $V$. Let $v,w\in K$ and $\theta\in[0,1]$ and set $u:= \theta v+(1-\theta)w\in V$. Then we have
	\begin{equation*}
		u_\mathrm{n} =(\gamma u)\cdot \mathrm{n} = (\theta\gamma v+(1-\theta)\gamma w)\cdot \mathrm{n} = \theta(\gamma v\cdot \mathrm{n})+(1-\theta)(\gamma w\cdot\mathrm{n}) = \theta v_\mathrm{n}+(1-\theta)w_\mathrm{n}
	\end{equation*}
	and $v_\mathrm{n},w_\mathrm{n}\le 0$ a.e.\ on $\Gamma_C$ and $\theta,(1-\theta)\ge 0$. Thus,
	\begin{equation*}
		\theta v_\mathrm{n}+(1-\theta)w_\mathrm{n}\le 0\qquad\text{a.e.\ on }\Gamma_C,
	\end{equation*}
	i.e.\ $u\in K$, which makes $K$ convex. To prove that $K$ is closed, let $(v_k)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset K$ with
	$v_k\to v$ in $V$. Since the trace operator $\gamma:V\to H^{1/2}(\Gamma;\Omega)$
	is continuous, the mapping
	\begin{equation*}
		T:V\to L^2(\Gamma_C),\qquad T(w):=((\gamma w)\cdot \mathrm{n})|_{\Gamma_C}=w_\mathrm{n}|_{\Gamma_C},
	\end{equation*}
	is continuous as well. Hence,
	\begin{equation*}
		(v_k)_\mathrm{n}=T(v_k)\to T(v)=v_\mathrm{n}
		\qquad\text{in }L^2(\Gamma_C).
	\end{equation*}
	Since $(v_k)_\mathrm{n}\le 0$ a.e.\ on $\Gamma_C$ for all $k$, there exists a
	subsequence $((v_{k_j})_\mathrm{n})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}$ such that
	\begin{equation*}
		(v_{k_j})_\mathrm{n}(x)\to v_\mathrm{n}(x)
		\qquad\text{for a.e. }x\in\Gamma_C.
	\end{equation*}
	Passing to the limit in the inequality $(v_{k_j})_\mathrm{n}(x)\le 0$, we obtain
	\begin{equation*}
		v_\mathrm{n}(x)\le 0
		\qquad\text{for a.e. }x\in\Gamma_C.
	\end{equation*}
	Thus $v\in K$, so $K$ is closed.
\end{proof}
